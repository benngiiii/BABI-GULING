## 3. Review Findings and Suggested Improvements

### QR-205-I01 — Review Findings

#### Finding 1: Relationship Between Personal Data and PII

Section 2.1 describes personal data as also being called personally identifiable information (PII).

These terms are related, but their definitions and legal usage can differ between jurisdictions.

**Suggested improvement:**

Explain that personal data is the terminology used in frameworks such as the GDPR, while PII is commonly used in other privacy contexts. Avoid presenting them as universally identical terms.

**Result:** Passed. The explanation introduces the relationship between the concepts. A short clarification would further improve terminology precision.

#### Finding 2: Explanation of Anonymisation

The chapter correctly explains that anonymised data may sometimes be re-identified.

Learners could benefit from a clearer distinction between removing direct identifiers and achieving effective anonymisation.

**Suggested improvement:**

Add a short example showing that removing a name or student ID does not necessarily prevent identification when other details can be combined.

**Result:** Passed. The chapter provides a suitable introduction to anonymisation and its limitations. The additional example is an optional improvement.

#### Finding 3: Scope of the Four-Fifths Rule

The explanation of the four-fifths rule is generally correct, and the calculations are consistent.

The chapter introduces the rule as a way to screen for potential differences in selection rates.

**Suggested improvement:**

Emphasise that this is a preliminary screening measure associated with US employment-selection guidance, not a universal legal definition of fairness. Its interpretation depends on the legal and practical context, and additional analysis may be necessary.

**Result:** Passed. The calculation and its educational purpose are suitable for the chapter.

#### Finding 4: Distinction Between Bias and Hallucination

Section 5.2 compares bias and hallucination effectively.

These concepts represent different types of AI problems: bias concerns unfair or systematically unequal outcomes, while hallucination concerns false, fabricated, or unsupported information.

**Suggested improvement:**

A short comparison table could reinforce this distinction:

| Concept       | Main Concern                                       | Example                                            |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Bias          | Unequal or systematically less favourable outcomes | A hiring system rejects one group more frequently. |
| Hallucination | False, fabricated, or unsupported information      | A chatbot invents a research paper.                |

**Result:** Passed. The chapter explains both concepts clearly. The comparison table is an optional learning enhancement.

#### Finding 5: Study-Time Breakdown

The estimated study time is approximately 3–4 hours.

A more detailed time allocation for all five topics and their activities could help learners plan their study sessions.

**Suggested improvement:**

Add an approximate study-time breakdown covering reading, worked examples, activities, and discussion. Ensure the total remains consistent with the stated 3–4 hours.

**Result:** Passed. The stated study duration provides an appropriate overall estimate. A detailed breakdown is optional.

#### Finding 6: Bias Diagram and Visual Materials

The chapter references:

`assets/bias-occurence-diagram.png`

The visual material supports the explanation of the AI bias life cycle.

**Suggested improvement:**

Maintain the diagram as supporting material and ensure its filename and reference remain consistent. If the asset is renamed, update the Markdown path accordingly. The spelling `occurence` may be corrected to `occurrence` if the filename is changed.

**Result:** Passed. The visual material is considered suitable for supporting the chapter explanation.

#### Finding 7: Final Reflection Assessment Criteria

The final reflection asks learners to write approximately 150–200 words and lists the expected content.

This provides a useful direction for learners.

**Suggested improvement:**

Add a short assessment checklist covering:

* Identification of responsible people or organisations.
* Application of at least two responsible AI principles.
* Explanation of human oversight.
* A practical prevention or improvement measure.

**Result:** Passed. The reflection provides a meaningful opportunity for learners to apply the chapter concepts. The checklist is an optional assessment improvement.

#### Finding 8: Human and Organisational Responsibility

The statement that AI systems do not have human intentions or moral responsibility provides a useful starting point.

The chapter could further distinguish between the AI system's role and the legal or professional responsibilities of the people and organisations involved.

**Suggested improvement:**

Clarify that responsibility depends on the roles, decisions, and applicable duties of developers, deployers, organisations, and users. Avoid suggesting that every person involved automatically carries the same responsibility.

**Result:** Passed. The chapter introduces the importance of human responsibility in AI use. Further clarification would strengthen the discussion.

#### Finding 9: References and External Sources

The chapter includes relevant academic papers, institutional frameworks, regulations, and legal cases.

These sources support the main subject areas and provide useful starting points for further learning.

The intentionally fabricated Henderson & Zhou (2019) citation is clearly identified as a teaching example and should remain excluded from the actual reference list.

**Result:** Passed. The reference section is relevant and appropriate for the chapter's educational purpose.

**Result:** Passed. The learner-perspective evaluation is documented in this report.

## 5. Positive Findings

1. **Strong chapter structure:** The chapter progresses logically from AI limitations to practical responsibilities and real-world discussion.

2. **Good connection with previous chapters:** The opening recap connects AI applications from Chapter 4 with the ethical concerns discussed in Chapter 5.

3. **Clear learning outcomes:** The outcomes use measurable actions such as explain, identify, calculate, recognise, and apply.

4. **Practical explanation of bias:** The life-cycle table helps learners understand that bias can enter during data collection, labelling, training, testing, and deployment.

5. **Useful mathematical application:** The selection-rate and impact-ratio examples provide a practical way to introduce fairness screening.

6. **Good privacy scenario:** The clinic-letter example connects privacy concepts with a realistic situation involving sensitive information.

7. **Practical hallucination verification routine:** The five-step process gives learners a useful method for checking AI-generated claims and citations.

8. **Responsible AI checklist:** The checklist translates abstract principles into questions that learners can apply to real situations.

9. **Relevant real-world cases:** Gender Shades, healthcare risk prediction, Amazon's recruiting tool, and Mata v. Avianca help demonstrate why these issues matter.

10. **Comprehensive practice materials:** The chapter includes True/False, Multiple Choice, and Open-Ended questions with answers and explanations.

11. **Good distinction between concepts:** The chapter explains that bias and hallucination are different problems and that a system can experience either or both.

12. **Appropriate beginner level:** Programming is optional, technical terms are explained, and learners can complete the main activities without programming knowledge.

13. **Meaningful learning activities:** The activities encourage learners to investigate, calculate, discuss, and apply responsible AI principles.

14. **Suitable for independent study:** The chapter provides explanations, practical examples, activities, and answers that support self-directed learning.

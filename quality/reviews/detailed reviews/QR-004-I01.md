### General Assessment

Chapter 4 provides a comprehensive and beginner-friendly introduction to practical applications of Artificial Intelligence.

The chapter successfully covers healthcare, education, transportation, everyday life, and finance and trading. It uses familiar examples to explain different AI tasks, including classification, prediction, recommendation, anomaly detection, and automated decision-making.

## 3. Review Findings and Suggested Improvements

### Finding 1 — Distinction Between AI and Rule-Based Automation

**Location:** Topic 2.3, Topic 4.2, Topic 5.2, and Teacher Notes
**Priority:** Medium
**Status:** Open

**Finding:**

The chapter uses simplified examples to demonstrate how computer systems make decisions.

These include:

* An adaptive quiz that changes difficulty according to fixed rules.
* A spam filter that classifies messages using predefined word scores.
* A trading system that generates signals using moving averages.

These examples are useful for beginners because the calculations and decisions are easy to follow.

However, the chapter is specifically about AI applications, and learners may assume that all automated systems using predefined rules are examples of machine-learning-based AI.

The Teacher Notes already clarify that the trading example uses a fixed algorithm rather than a learning model, but this distinction is not consistently explained throughout the chapter.

**Suggested Improvement:**

Add a short clarification explaining that the simplified examples demonstrate automated decision-making rather than fully developed machine-learning systems.

For example:

> The following example uses fixed rules to demonstrate how automated decisions work. In real AI applications, machine-learning models may learn patterns from data instead of relying only on manually defined rules.

For Topic 4, clarify that the spam-filter scores are manually assigned for teaching purposes.

For Topic 5, explain that moving-average trading rules are examples of algorithmic automation and do not necessarily involve AI.

**Expected Result:** Learners can distinguish conventional rule-based automation from machine-learning-based AI while still understanding the purpose of the simplified examples.

### Finding 2 — Driving Automation and Level 3 Explanation

**Location:** Topic 3.3 — Levels of Driving Automation; Topic 3.4 — Learner Activity
**Priority:** Medium
**Status:** Open

**Finding:**

The chapter provides a useful overview of the SAE J3016 driving automation levels.

The distinction between Levels 0–2 and Levels 3–5 is generally explained correctly.

However, the Level 3 learner activity includes a scenario in which a driver may look at a phone while the vehicle operates.

This wording may suggest that phone use is universally permitted during Level 3 automated driving.

In practice, permitted driver activities depend on the system's operating conditions and applicable laws.

The scenario should also emphasize that the driver must remain available to respond to a takeover request.

**Suggested Improvement:**

Revise the Level 3 scenario to:

> In a traffic jam on a motorway, an approved Level 3 automated driving system performs the driving task within its operating conditions. The driver does not need to continuously monitor the road but must remain available to take over when requested.

The expected answer remains Level 3.

The Level 4 explanation may also clarify that the automated system does not require a human fallback driver while operating within its specified conditions.

**Expected Result:** Students understand the distinction between driver assistance and automated driving without receiving an overly broad impression of permitted driver behaviour.

### Finding 3 — Healthcare Screening and Responsibility Statements

**Location:** Topic 1.1 — Why Healthcare?; Topic 1.7 — Limits and Responsibilities
**Priority:** Low
**Status:** Open

**Finding:**

The Healthcare topic clearly introduces medical imaging, screening, diagnosis, and clinical decision support.

However, some definitions and statements are expressed too absolutely.

For example:

* Screening is defined only as testing people without symptoms.
* Clinical decision support is described as software where the clinician always makes the final decision.
* Responsibility is described as remaining with a named clinician or organisation.

These statements communicate the importance of human oversight, but actual clinical workflows and responsibility arrangements can vary.

**Suggested Improvement:**

Use more qualified wording:

* Screening commonly involves testing people without symptoms to identify possible disease early.
* Clinical decision support provides information or recommendations to assist healthcare professionals.
* Responsibility and oversight depend on the clinical workflow, the type of tool, and applicable regulations.

**Expected Result:** The chapter maintains beginner-friendly explanations while avoiding statements that may not apply universally.

### Finding 4 — Education AI Capabilities and Limitations

**Location:** Topic 2.1 — What Does AI Do in Education?; Topic 2.4 — Learner Activity
**Priority:** Low
**Status:** Open

**Finding:**

The Education topic effectively explains adaptive learning, intelligent tutoring systems, and learning analytics.

The worked example and learner activity are also clear.

However, the answer to the learner activity suggests that AI systems cannot notice emotional distress or explain an idea in a new way.

Some modern AI systems can generate alternative explanations or detect certain behavioural signals, although their interpretations may be unreliable.

The important limitation is that AI cannot reliably understand a learner's complete emotional, social, and personal circumstances or replace the broader responsibilities of a teacher.

**Suggested Improvement:**

Revise the model answer to emphasize that AI can provide adaptive practice and alternative explanations but cannot reliably replace teachers' judgment, emotional support, classroom management, and responsibility for student wellbeing.

The discussion of tutoring effectiveness should also clarify that research findings vary across subjects, systems, and study conditions.

**Expected Result:** The chapter presents a balanced explanation of what educational AI can and cannot reliably do.

### Finding 5 — Trading Rule and Initial Buy Signal

**Location:** Topic 5.2 — Worked Example: A Simple Trading Rule
**Priority:** Low
**Status:** Open

**Finding:**

The moving-average calculations and trading results are consistent with the assumptions stated in the chapter.

The example correctly shows a buy at 11, a sell at 9, and a loss of 2 per unit.

However, the rule describes buying when the short-term moving average "moves above" the long-term moving average.

This wording normally suggests a crossover from a previous state where the short-term average was not above the long-term average.

In the worked example, Day 3 is the first day when both moving averages can be calculated. Therefore, an actual crossover from an earlier day cannot be observed.

The example treats the first available observation of the short-term average being above the long-term average as an initial buy signal.

**Suggested Improvement:**

Clarify the teaching assumption:

> For this simplified example, the first day when both moving averages are available counts as a buy signal if the short-term average is above the long-term average. After that, new signals occur when their relative positions change.

The existing calculations and answer key can remain unchanged.

**Expected Result:** The trading rule is defined consistently with the worked example.

### Finding 8 — Estimated Learning Time and Suggested Pacing

**Location:** Chapter Header and Teacher Notes
**Priority:** Low
**Status:** Open

**Finding:**

The chapter estimates a total learning time of four hours.

However, the Teacher Notes suggest:

* Healthcare: 60–75 minutes.
* Education, Transportation, Everyday Life, and Finance: approximately 30–40 minutes each.
* Synthesis Activity: 20 minutes.

This gives a total estimated duration of approximately 200–255 minutes.

The upper end exceeds the stated four-hour estimate.

**Suggested Improvement:**

Either:

* Adjust the overall estimate to approximately 3.5–4.5 hours, or
* Adjust the suggested pacing so that the total fits within four hours.

**Expected Result:** The estimated learning time and teacher guidance are consistent.

### Finding 9 — Precision and Disease Prevalence Explanation

**Location:** Topic 1.5 — Worked Example: Evaluating a Screening Tool
**Priority:** Low / Optional
**Status:** Optional Improvement

**Finding:**

The healthcare worked example correctly demonstrates that precision can decrease when disease prevalence decreases, even when sensitivity and specificity remain unchanged.

The calculations for both scenarios are accurate.

However, the relationship between prevalence and false positives may be difficult for some beginners to understand immediately.

**Suggested Improvement:**

Consider adding:

> When a disease is rare, there are many more healthy people than affected people. Even a small false-positive rate can therefore produce more false alarms than true detections.

This is an optional improvement because the existing worked example already demonstrates the concept correctly.

**Expected Result:** Improved understanding of why high accuracy does not always mean that positive predictions are reliable.

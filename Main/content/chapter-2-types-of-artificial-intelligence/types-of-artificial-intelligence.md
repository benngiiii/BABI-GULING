## 2.1 Introduction to AI Classification

In Chapter 1, we learned that Artificial Intelligence (AI) allows computers to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence. We also learned that AI can recognize images, answer questions, and recommend videos.

However, not all AI systems have the same abilities. Some are designed for a specific task, while others can handle many different types of tasks. Some use information from earlier observations, while others respond mainly to the information available at the present moment.

To understand these differences, we use classification systems.

A classification system is simply a way to group things based on shared features. For example, animals can be grouped according to their habitats or diets. In a similar way, AI can be grouped according to its abilities and behavior.

This chapter introduces two commonly discussed ways to classify AI:

1. Classification by capability: How broadly can an AI system perform intellectual tasks?
2. Classification by functionality: How does an AI system behave or use information?

These frameworks help us describe AI, but they do not cover every aspect of every AI system. They are also not one universally accepted scientific classification (IBM, n.d.).

## 2.2 AI Based on Capability

Capability refers to the range and level of tasks an AI system can perform. Under this framework, AI is commonly divided into three categories:

- Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI)
- Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)
- Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)

These categories describe different levels of possible capability. They should not be understood as three equally developed technologies.

### 2.2.1 Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI)

Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI) is AI designed to perform a specific task or a limited range of tasks.

Think about a calculator. It can perform calculations quickly, but it cannot automatically become a doctor, write a novel, or drive a car. An AI system designed for a particular purpose also has limits, even when it performs that purpose very well.

ANI is sometimes called narrow AI or weak AI. The word weak does not mean that the system is poor at its task. It means that its abilities are limited in scope compared with the broad abilities associated with human intelligence.

Examples of ANI

- Facial recognition: An AI system can compare a face with stored information to help identify a person.
- Spam detection: An AI system can identify suspicious emails and move them into a spam folder.
- Recommendation systems: A video platform may suggest content based on a user's activity.
- Conversational AI: An assistant may answer questions, summarize text, and help with writing.

These systems perform different tasks, but they do not automatically possess general human-level intelligence.

Main characteristics of ANI

- It is designed for a specific task or range of tasks.
- It may perform its intended task faster or more accurately than a human.
- It can still make mistakes.
- Strong performance in one area does not prove that it can perform every intellectual task.

Current AI applications, including modern conversational systems, are generally considered narrow AI under this framework. Their ability to handle many different requests does not, by itself, establish that they have achieved AGI (Stanford HAI, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

### 2.2.2 Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) refers to a proposed form of AI that could learn, reason, and apply knowledge across a broad range of intellectual tasks at a level comparable to humans.

Imagine a person who learns to cook and then uses their general problem-solving skills to learn a new language, repair a bicycle, or plan a journey. People can transfer knowledge between different situations. AGI aims for similarly broad and flexible abilities in an artificial system.

AGI does not need to look like a human or have a human body. The main idea is the breadth and flexibility of its intellectual abilities.

What might AGI be able to do?

A system that meets a strong definition of AGI might be able to:

- Learn unfamiliar intellectual tasks with limited task-specific preparation.
- Apply knowledge from one area to another.
- Solve new problems it has not encountered before.
- Adapt to different situations with broad flexibility.

These are proposed characteristics. Researchers do not all agree on exactly which abilities are necessary for AGI.

Does AGI exist today?

There is no universally accepted definition or test for AGI. Researchers and technology organizations may disagree about which abilities are necessary to qualify.

Current AI systems can perform many impressive tasks. However, broad performance alone does not settle whether a system meets a particular definition of AGI (Stanford HAI, n.d.-b).

For this chapter, AGI is treated as a proposed level of general intelligence, not as an achievement that has been universally confirmed.

### 2.2.3 Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)

Artificial Superintelligence (ASI) refers to a hypothetical form of AI that would surpass human intellectual abilities across many or most important domains.

The difference between AGI and ASI can be understood simply:

- AGI: Broad intellectual abilities comparable to humans.
- ASI: Intellectual abilities that surpass humans across many important areas.

For example, a hypothetical ASI might solve difficult scientific problems, develop new mathematical ideas, and plan complex projects at levels beyond the best human experts.

These are possible future scenarios, not descriptions of an AI system known to exist today.

Possible benefits and concerns

If such a system were developed, it might help researchers investigate difficult scientific questions or find solutions to complex problems. However, it could also raise serious concerns about safety, control, responsibility, and the effects of its decisions on people.

Its effects would depend on how it was designed, managed, and used. It would be inaccurate to assume that superintelligence would automatically be helpful or harmful.

Does ASI exist today?

ASI remains hypothetical. There is no verified evidence that an AI system currently surpasses human intellectual abilities across all or nearly all relevant domains.

High performance also does not automatically imply consciousness, emotions, or self-awareness. Those are separate questions.

### 2.2.4 Comparing ANI, AGI, and ASI

| Feature | ANI | AGI | ASI |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main idea | Specific or limited tasks | Broad, human-comparable intellectual abilities | Intellectual abilities beyond humans across many domains |
| Scope | Limited to its designed or learned range of tasks | Broad and flexible | Hypothetically broader and more capable than human intelligence |
| Status | Exists today | No universally accepted confirmation of achievement | Hypothetical |
| Example | Spam filter or facial recognition system | A hypothetical system with broad, flexible learning | A hypothetical system that surpasses human experts across many fields |

Key takeaway: ANI is established in practical AI applications today. AGI and ASI describe broader levels of possible capability whose achievement has not been universally established.

## 2.3 AI Based on Functionality

The second framework groups AI according to its behavior and use of information. A commonly taught framework contains four categories:

1. Reactive Machines
2. Limited-Memory AI
3. Theory-of-Mind AI
4. Self-Aware AI

These categories are useful for introductory learning, but they are not a universally standardized technical taxonomy. Real AI systems may be more complex than the simple examples used to explain them.

### 2.3.1 Reactive Machines

Reactive machines are systems that respond to the information available at the present moment without using stored memories of past events to guide their decisions in the way described by this category.

A classic example is IBM's Deep Blue chess computer, which defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997. Deep Blue evaluated chess positions and selected moves using its computational methods. It is commonly used to illustrate reactive AI because it did not learn from a personal memory of previous games during play.

Deep Blue was technically complex, so it should not be mistaken for a simple fixed-rule machine.

Main characteristics

- Responds to current inputs.
- Does not rely on stored personal experiences from earlier situations in the way described by the limited-memory category.
- Can perform a specific task very well.
- Does not automatically gain broader abilities outside its intended task.

Advantages

Reactive systems can be useful for tasks with clear goals and defined conditions. Their behavior may be easier to analyze when their operating methods are well understood.

Limitations

A purely reactive system cannot use a stored history of its own previous situations in the way that a system with memory can. It may therefore be unsuitable for tasks that depend heavily on remembering earlier events.

### 2.3.2 Limited-Memory AI

Limited-Memory AI refers to systems that use information from earlier observations or stored data when producing an output or making a decision.

Imagine a driver approaching a busy road. The driver observes nearby cars and remembers what happened moments earlier to decide whether to slow down. An AI driving system may similarly use recent observations to track traffic and help make decisions.

Examples

- Driver-assistance systems: Some systems use recent sensor observations to track nearby vehicles.
- Recommendation systems: Some use a person's earlier viewing or listening activity to suggest content.
- Conversational AI: A system may use earlier messages in the same conversation to make a reply more relevant.

These examples illustrate different ways of using past information. Not every recommendation system or chatbot has the same memory design.

Main characteristics

- Uses previous observations or stored information in some form.
- Can consider changes over time when its design supports this.
- May make more context-sensitive decisions than a system using only one isolated input.
- Remains limited by the information it can access and the way it was designed.

An important clarification about memory

Limited memory does not always mean that a system remembers only for a few seconds. Some systems use information during a short interaction, while others can use historical records over longer periods.

Also, a system that uses stored information does not necessarily learn or improve automatically every time it is used. Updating a model and using existing information to produce a response are different things.

Many modern AI applications use past data or contextual information in some form, although their designs vary considerably (IBM, n.d.).

### 2.3.3 Theory-of-Mind AI

Theory-of-Mind AI is a proposed category of AI that would understand other people's beliefs, intentions, emotions, and perspectives in a deeper and more flexible way.

In everyday life, people try to understand what others know or feel. For example, a teacher may notice that a student is confused even when the student does not say so directly. The teacher can then change the explanation to help the student.

A future AI with a strong theory-of-mind ability would ideally reason about another person's mental state and use that understanding appropriately.

What might it do?

A hypothetical system might:

- Recognize that two people have different beliefs about the same situation.
- Consider what another person knows or does not know.
- Adjust an explanation to match a person's perspective.
- Reason about possible intentions while recognizing uncertainty.

These examples describe a proposed capability, not proof that current AI systems possess it in the full human sense.

Does this type of AI exist today?

AI systems can recognize certain emotional cues, classify facial expressions, or generate responses that sound empathetic. However, these abilities do not establish that a system genuinely understands another person's inner experience.

Full theory-of-mind AI, as described here, remains an unestablished capability.

Main limitation

Human emotions and intentions are complex. The same expression can mean different things in different situations. An AI system may misinterpret someone's behavior, words, or cultural background.

Therefore, we should not assume that an AI understands a person's feelings simply because it produces a convincing response.

### 2.3.4 Self-Aware AI

Self-aware AI is a hypothetical form of AI that would possess awareness of its own existence and internal state in a meaningful sense.

Consider the difference between a machine that reports its battery level and a being that is aware of itself as an individual. A machine can monitor its battery and report that it is low without being conscious of its own existence.

This distinction is important when discussing self-aware AI.

What might self-aware AI involve?

A hypothetical self-aware system might possess:

- A meaningful awareness of itself as an entity.
- An understanding of its own internal states.
- Some form of subjective experience, if consciousness is part of the definition being used.

There is no single agreed scientific definition of self-aware AI. These proposed features should not be treated as a confirmed technical specification.

Does self-aware AI exist today?

There is no widely accepted scientific evidence that current AI systems possess consciousness or self-awareness in this strong sense.

An AI system may describe itself, monitor its own performance, or say phrases such as "I think" and "I feel." These statements alone do not prove that it has subjective experiences or a conscious sense of self.

Main limitation

Consciousness is difficult to define and measure. It is therefore difficult to establish what evidence would be sufficient to prove machine consciousness. Claims about self-aware AI should be made carefully.

### 2.3.5 Comparing the Four Functionality Categories

| Category | Main idea | Illustrative example | Current status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reactive Machines | Respond to current information without using stored past experiences in the described way | Deep Blue's chess-playing system | Classic examples exist |
| Limited-Memory AI | Uses past observations or stored information | A driving system tracking nearby vehicles | Common in current AI applications |
| Theory-of-Mind AI | Would reason about beliefs, intentions, and perspectives | A hypothetical assistant that understands what another person knows or intends | Full capability remains unestablished |
| Self-Aware AI | Would possess meaningful self-awareness or consciousness | A hypothetical conscious machine | Hypothetical; no accepted demonstration |

The table describes the categories as used in this introductory framework. It does not mean that every system assigned to a category shares the same design or abilities.

## 2.4 Comparing the Two Classification Frameworks

The capability framework and the functionality framework answer different questions.

- Capability classification asks how broadly an AI system can perform intellectual tasks.
- Functionality classification describes how an AI system behaves, uses information, or is conceptualized in relation to features such as memory and awareness.

These frameworks are not interchangeable. An AI system's ability to perform many tasks does not, by itself, prove that it has memory in a particular sense, understands human emotions, or is conscious.

For example, a conversational AI may answer science questions, write stories, and summarize documents. These abilities show that it can handle a range of tasks. They do not establish that it is AGI, understands emotions as a human does, or is self-aware.

### A Simple Comparison

| Question | Capability framework | Functionality framework |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does it focus on? | Breadth of intellectual ability | Behavior, use of information, or proposed cognitive features |
| What are the categories? | ANI, AGI, ASI | Reactive, limited-memory, theory-of-mind, self-aware |
| What does it help us understand? | How broad or advanced an AI's abilities are intended to be | How the system is described in terms of response, memory, or proposed awareness |
| Does it prove consciousness? | No | No; consciousness is only part of the proposed self-aware category |

These frameworks provide different ways to discuss AI. They do not guarantee that every real system fits neatly into one category.

## 2.5 Common Misunderstandings About AI Types

### Misunderstanding 1: Every AI system has the same abilities.

Reality: AI systems can be designed for very different tasks. A spam filter and a conversational assistant have different purposes and capabilities.

### Misunderstanding 2: A powerful chatbot must be AGI.

Reality: A chatbot may handle many kinds of requests, but broad performance alone does not settle whether it meets a definition of AGI. Researchers disagree about the precise criteria.

### Misunderstanding 3: An AI that talks about emotions must feel them.

Reality: Producing language about emotions is not proof of having emotional experiences. A system can generate a sympathetic sentence without that sentence demonstrating consciousness.

### Misunderstanding 4: All four functionality categories already exist.

Reality: Reactive and limited-memory systems have practical examples. Full theory-of-mind and self-aware AI remain unestablished in the senses described in this chapter.

### Misunderstanding 5: The two classification frameworks mean the same thing.

Reality: Capability classification concerns the breadth of abilities. Functionality classification concerns behavior, information use, and proposed cognitive features.

### Part B: Apply the Ideas

Q6. A company creates an AI system that detects damaged products on a factory conveyor belt. Which capability category best describes this system, and why?

Q7. A driving system uses recent observations of nearby vehicles to help decide when to slow down. Which functionality category does this illustrate?

Q8. Imagine a future AI that could reliably consider what different people believe, what information they have, and why they may act differently. Which functionality category would this resemble? Is this capability established in current AI?

Q9. A conversational assistant can write stories, answer questions, and summarize reports. Does this automatically make it AGI? Explain why or why not.

Q10. Explain in your own words why capability classification and functionality classification are different.

### Answers and Explanations

A1. B. ANI is designed for specific or limited tasks, while AGI refers to broad intellectual abilities across different tasks and domains.

A2. C. ASI describes a hypothetical AI that would surpass human intellectual abilities across many or most relevant domains.

A3. There is no universally accepted definition or test for AGI. A system may perform well on many tasks without satisfying every proposed definition of general intelligence.

A4. A reactive machine responds to current information without relying on stored past experiences in the way described by this category. Limited-memory AI can use earlier observations or stored information to guide its output or decisions.

A5. No. The ability to produce words about emotions does not prove that a system actually experiences them. Language output and consciousness are different questions.

A6. ANI. The system is designed for a specific task: detecting damaged products. Performing that task well does not establish general intelligence.

A7. Limited-Memory AI. The example uses recent observations over time to help make a decision.

A8. Theory-of-Mind AI. The example describes the proposed ability to reason about people's beliefs and perspectives. A complete capability of this kind has not been established in current AI.

A9. No. Handling several kinds of requests does not automatically establish AGI. The system must be assessed against a clear definition of general intelligence, and there is no universally accepted test.

A10. Capability classification describes how broadly an AI system could perform intellectual tasks. Functionality classification describes behavior, information use, or proposed features such as understanding other minds or self-awareness.

## 2.7 Summary and References

### Key Takeaways

- AI can be classified in different ways because AI systems have different abilities and characteristics.
- The capability framework contains ANI, AGI, and ASI.
- ANI is established in current practical AI applications, while AGI and ASI remain subjects of debate and hypothetical future development.
- The functionality framework commonly describes reactive machines, limited-memory AI, theory-of-mind AI, and self-aware AI.
- Reactive and limited-memory systems have practical examples. Full theory-of-mind and self-aware AI remain unestablished in the senses described here.
- An AI system that speaks fluently or performs many tasks does not automatically possess general intelligence, emotions, or consciousness.
- Capability and functionality are different ways of discussing AI. Neither framework explains every aspect of a system.

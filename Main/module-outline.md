## Why the Structure Changed

The first Stage 1 version used five chapters chosen by the team. The lecturer's feedback required the module to follow the course materials (PPT2024). The module now has **four chapters, one for each course chapter provided by the lecturer**. Each chapter takes its title and its topic order from the lecturer's slides.

| Our chapter | Title | Course source (lecturer's slides) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Introduction to CS and AI | chap1: Intro to CS and AI |
| 2 | Symbolic AI | chap4: Symbolic AI |
| 3 | Connectionist AI and Big Data | chap5: Connectionist AI and Big Data Overview |
| 4 | Behaviorist AI | chap6: Behaviorist AI |

Chapters 2 to 4 follow the order in which the course teaches the three classic approaches. Chapter 1 gives the shared background.

## Module Learning Outcomes

After completing this module, learners should be able to:

1. Define Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, describe their history and main areas, and explain the three families of machine learning.
2. Explain Symbolic AI: logic, knowledge representation, rule-based and expert systems, planning and search, and symbolic natural language processing, with its benefits and limits.
3. Explain Connectionist AI (neurons, learning, architectures, deep learning) and Big Data (the Vs, technologies, processing, visualization, ethics).
4. Explain Behaviorist AI: reinforcement learning, Markov decision processes, algorithms such as Q-learning, applications in robotics and games, and its challenges.
5. Compare the three approaches, match them to suitable applications, and identify typical risks of each (wrong rules, biased data, reward hacking, privacy).

Chapter 1 supports outcome 1, Chapter 2 outcome 2, Chapter 3 outcome 3, and Chapter 4 outcome 4. Outcome 5 is supported by the comparison sections in Chapters 3 and 4.

## Prerequisites

No prior knowledge of AI or programming is required. Read the chapters in order. Chapter 1 gives the vocabulary. Chapter 2 introduces symbols and rules. Chapter 3 builds on Chapter 2 by showing the learning alternative. Chapter 4 introduces learning from reward and ends with a comparison of the three approaches.

## Planned Examples and Activities

| Chapter | Outcome | Activity | What learners do | Expected outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Rock Paper Scissors and Smart Classroom (machine learning); ask-and-verify exercise | Train a classifier with labelled examples; check AI-assistant answers against the chapter | A model learns from labelled examples, and poor examples give poor results |
| 2 | 2 | Rule-based Smart Classroom | Write IF-THEN rules for fan and lamp commands and test new wording | Rules are clear and explainable but fail on wording that no rule covers |
| 3 | 3 | Compute a neuron; make a perceptron compute OR; machine-learning Smart Classroom; check a data claim | Calculate outputs by hand; train a text classifier; trace a statistic to its source | Weights learned from data handle new wording; data claims must be verified |
| 4 | 4 | Train the corridor by hand (Q-learning); spot the reward hack; Rock Paper Scissors against a learner | Update Q-values; find loopholes in rewards | Reward spreads backward along a path; badly designed rewards can be exploited |

The same Smart Classroom task is used in Chapters 1, 2, and 3 so that learners see rules and learning solve one problem in different ways.
